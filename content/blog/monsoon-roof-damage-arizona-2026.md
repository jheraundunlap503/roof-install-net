# Monsoon Roof Damage in Arizona: 2026 Season Guide

**The 2026 monsoon has already produced measured wind gusts of 84 mph in the West Valley, with roof sections torn off, carports destroyed, and more than 50,000 homes losing power in a single August night. Most monsoon roof damage in Arizona is wind damage, not rain damage, and it often looks like nothing from the ground. The season runs through September 30, so there is still time for another event, and still time to get a roof looked at before the trailing storms arrive.**

Arizona monsoon damage does not look like hurricane footage. It looks like three lifted tiles on a back slope and a stain that shows up on a bedroom ceiling six weeks later. Knowing what actually happened this season helps you know what to look for.

## What monsoon roof damage has Arizona seen in 2026?

Two documented events stand out so far, both from the National Weather Service.

**August 9, Phoenix metro.** A thunderstorm cluster tracked westward along Interstate 10 through Tolleson, Litchfield Park, Avondale, Goodyear, and Buckeye. Per the [NWS summary of the August 9 wind event](https://www.weather.gov/psr/August92026), Phoenix Terminal Doppler Radar sampled winds exceeding 70 to 80 mph, and ground observations confirmed it. A weather station in Buckeye recorded a peak gust of 84 mph, and Buckeye Municipal Airport recorded 73 mph.

The damage was structural, not cosmetic. NWS documented "destroyed carports, blown-in garage doors, and roof parts blown off," with large trees uprooted and power poles snapped. Salt River Project and Arizona Public Service reported more than 50,000 customers without power at the height of the storm. The worst of it hit Avondale, where winds lifted and rolled metal construction pipes weighing at least 1,000 pounds each into a neighboring area.

**August 12, western Arizona.** A separate system brought [damaging winds, a dust storm, and flash flooding](https://www.weather.gov/psr/August122026) to the Imperial Valley and Quartzsite, with radar-estimated rainfall of 2 to 4 inches around Quartzsite and peak gusts near 59 mph at regional airports.

The NWS keeps a running index of these at its [event summaries page](https://www.weather.gov/psr/eventsummaries), which is worth checking against the date you think your damage occurred. An insurer will ask.

## Why is monsoon damage usually wind damage, not rain damage?

Because the wind arrives first and does the structural work.

A monsoon cell hits with an outflow boundary ahead of the rain. That gust front is what lifts tile, breaks the seal on shingle courses, peels flashing, and tears at roof edges. The rain that follows is what finds the openings the wind just made.

This matters for what you inspect. Standard three-tab shingles are rated well below the gusts recorded in Buckeye this August, and even architectural shingles depend on their adhesive seal being intact. [Owens Corning's installation instructions](https://www.owenscorning.com/en-us/roofing/install-instructions/duration-series) specify fastening patterns and sealing conditions precisely because wind uplift is the failure mode those details exist to resist. A roof installed slightly out of spec can lose shingles at speeds a correctly installed roof survives.

Tile behaves differently. Tile rarely blows away, but wind lifts and shifts it, and a shifted tile exposes underlayment that was never meant to see direct sun or driven rain. The [NRCA's technical resources](https://www.nrca.net/roofing-guidelines/resources) treat underlayment as the actual water barrier, which is why a minor tile shift is not minor.

## What should you check after a monsoon storm?

Work from the inside out, because the interior tells you the truth soonest.

- **Ceilings and closets.** Fresh staining, bubbling paint, or a musty smell. Check the ceilings of rooms you rarely enter.
- **The attic, with a flashlight, during daylight.** Daylight through the deck, damp insulation, or dark streaks on rafters are unambiguous.
- **The ground.** Granules in downspout splash zones, tile fragments in the yard, or pieces of flashing on the patio.
- **The roof edge from the ground, with binoculars.** Lifted or misaligned tile, missing shingle tabs, displaced ridge caps, bent drip edge.
- **Rooftop equipment.** Evaporative coolers, satellite mounts, and solar attachments are common leak points after wind loading.

Do not walk the roof yourself after a storm. Wet tile breaks underfoot, and broken tile you caused is not storm damage your insurer will pay for. ## Is monsoon roof damage covered by insurance?

Wind damage is a covered peril on standard Arizona homeowners policies. That is the good news, and it is why the wind-versus-rain distinction matters so much.

What insurers dispute is causation and age. A 22 year old roof that lost tiles on August 9 invites the argument that deterioration, not wind, is the proximate cause. Documentation is how you win that argument: dated photos, the NWS event summary for that specific date, and an independent inspection before the adjuster arrives.

Damage that develops gradually, including long-term leaks and neglected maintenance, is excluded almost everywhere. So is damage from water entering through an opening the policy treats as pre-existing. Our guide to [roof damage insurance coverage in Arizona](/blog/roof-damage-insurance-coverage-arizona/) covers the exclusions in detail, and [how to file a roof insurance claim](/blog/how-to-file-roof-insurance-claim/) walks the process.

## Should you actually file a claim?

Not always, and this is where honest advice diverges from what a storm-chasing contractor will tell you.

Run the math first. If your wind and hail deductible is a percentage of dwelling coverage rather than a flat amount, it can reach several thousand dollars on a typical Phoenix-area home. Damage that costs $3,800 to repair is not a claim, it is a repair. Filing anyway puts a claim on your record without producing a payment.

File when the damage is clearly storm-caused, clearly exceeds your deductible, and is tied to a specific dated event. Repair out of pocket when it is a handful of tiles and a flashing detail. The middle ground, where a contractor insists the whole roof needs replacing and the adjuster sees a repair, is where most Arizona claim disputes live.

## How do you avoid storm chasers after a monsoon event?

They arrive within days of exactly the kind of storm Avondale and Buckeye saw this August, and the pitch is always urgency.

Verify licensing before anything else. The [Arizona Registrar of Contractors](https://roc.az.gov/before-hire) publishes license status, and an out-of-state crew working under a borrowed number is a pattern the ROC sees every monsoon season. If work goes wrong, your recourse runs through the [ROC complaint process](https://roc.az.gov/file-complaint), which requires that the contractor was licensed at the time of the work and imposes a two year filing deadline.

Refuse to sign anything at the door that assigns your insurance benefits to a contractor. Get the scope in writing. Confirm permit responsibility, since requirements vary between incorporated cities and [unincorporated Maricopa County](https://www.maricopa.gov/1674/Residential-Construction). ## When does the 2026 monsoon season end?

September 30. The [National Weather Service defines Arizona's monsoon season as June 15 through September 30](https://www.weather.gov/fgz/monsooninfo) every year, regardless of how active a given season turns out to be.

That leaves roughly six weeks of exposure from late August. September storms are typically fewer but not weaker, and a roof already loosened by the August 9 event is measurably more vulnerable to the next one. If you took wind this season and have not looked, the window to look is now, not October.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I prove my roof damage came from a specific monsoon storm?**
Photograph everything with timestamps, then match the date to the NWS event summary for your area. A documented 84 mph gust near your address on the date of your photos is far stronger evidence than a general claim that a storm came through.

**Can wind damage a tile roof without breaking any tiles?**
Yes, and this is the most commonly missed damage in Arizona. Wind lifts and shifts tile without cracking it. The tile looks fine from the street while the underlayment beneath sits exposed. It usually surfaces as a leak months later.

**How long do I have to file a monsoon damage claim?**
Policies set their own notice requirements, often measured in days rather than months, and delay is a common reason for denial. Report promptly even if you are undecided about pursuing repairs. Reporting is not the same as committing.

**Is monsoon damage the same as hail damage?**
No. Hail is impact damage that bruises and fractures. Monsoon damage in the low desert is predominantly wind, which lifts, shifts, and peels. They are inspected and documented differently. See our guide to [hail damage on Arizona roofs](/blog/hail-damage-roof-arizona/).

**Should I get an inspection if I did not see any damage?**
If you were in the August 9 corridor along Interstate 10 through Tolleson, Litchfield Park, Avondale, Goodyear, or Buckeye, yes. Damage at those wind speeds is frequently invisible from the ground, and finding it now costs less than finding it through a ceiling in January.
