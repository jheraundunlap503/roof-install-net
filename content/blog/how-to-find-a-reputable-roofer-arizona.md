# How to Find a Reputable Roofer in Arizona

**Finding a reputable Arizona roofer starts with the state license database, not a review site. The Registrar of Contractors publishes license status, classification, and complaint history for free, and it takes about two minutes to check. A roofer with active licensing in the correct classification, a bonded status, and no unresolved complaints has cleared a bar that star ratings cannot measure. Everything after that is about scope, paperwork, and whether they will still exist in five years when you need the warranty.**

Search for a roofer in Arizona and you get Yelp, Reddit threads, and Facebook group recommendations. Those tell you who was pleasant to deal with. They do not tell you who is licensed, who is bonded, or who has a pattern of complaints filed against them. The state publishes all three.

## How do you verify an Arizona roofer is licensed?

Go to the [Arizona Registrar of Contractors and search their license before you hire](https://roc.az.gov/before-hire). You need the license number or business name. The record shows whether the license is active, when it was issued, the classification held, bonding status, and any complaint history.

Three things to look at specifically:

- **Status must read active.** Suspended, revoked, and expired all mean the same thing to you: no state recourse if the work goes wrong.
- **The name on the license must match the name on your contract.** A crew "working under" someone else's license is the single most common Arizona roofing scam, and it leaves you with no protection.
- **Check the issue date.** A license issued three weeks ago on a company claiming twenty years of experience is a rebranded business, often one that dissolved to escape complaints.

## Does the license classification actually matter?

Yes, and this is the check most homeowners skip.

Arizona issues specific [contractor license classifications](https://roc.az.gov/license-classifications), and roofing work falls under its own. A general residential contractor license is not a roofing license. Someone can be entirely legitimate, fully licensed, and still not licensed for the work you are hiring them to do.

This matters at claim time. If a dispute reaches the ROC and the contractor was not licensed in the correct classification for roofing work, the situation changes for both of you. Our guide to [Arizona roofing contractor licensing](/blog/arizona-roofing-contractor-license/) breaks down which classifications cover what.

## What does the ROC complaint history tell you?

More than reviews do, because filing a complaint takes real effort.

A single resolved complaint on a high-volume roofer means little. A pattern of unresolved complaints, or complaints that closed because the homeowner ran out the clock, is a signal. The [ROC complaint process](https://roc.az.gov/file-complaint) is also your own future recourse, so it is worth understanding before you sign rather than after.

The deadline is strict. Per the ROC, for new construction complaints must be filed "within two years from the close of escrow or actual occupancy, whichever occurred first," and for remodel or repair work the two years run from the date the work was performed. A ten year workmanship warranty does not extend that window. It only gives you a private contract claim after the state's door closes.

Arizona also maintains a Residential Contractors' Recovery Fund with its own filing requirements, described in the [ROC's FAQ](https://roc.az.gov/faq). Both remedies require the contractor to have been licensed at the time of the work, which is the whole argument for checking first.

## Where do reviews and the BBB actually help?

After licensing, not before it.

The [Better Business Bureau's roofing directory](https://www.bbb.org/all/roofing) and its [Phoenix-area listings](https://www.bbb.org/us/az/phoenix) are useful for a different question than the ROC answers. The state tells you whether someone is legally qualified. The BBB and review platforms tell you how a company handles a customer who is unhappy, which is the thing you actually experience.

Read the negative reviews and ignore the star average. What you want to know is whether complaints get answered, whether the same problem repeats across reviewers, and whether the company responds like an adult. A roofer with forty reviews averaging 4.2 and thoughtful replies to the bad ones is a better bet than one with two hundred reviews averaging 4.9 and no replies at all.

## What separates a real bid from a sales pitch?

The paperwork, and it is obvious once you know what to look for.

A real bid names the specific product, the underlayment and its warranty, the fastening approach, the tear-off scope, who pulls the permit, and what happens if decking damage is found. A sales pitch names a price and a discount that expires. See [what a roofing estimate should include](/blog/what-does-roofing-estimate-include/) and [how to get roofing bids](/blog/how-to-get-roofing-bids/) for the comparison process, plus [estimate versus contract](/blog/roofing-estimate-vs-contract/) for what has to be in writing before work starts.

Permit responsibility deserves a direct question. Requirements differ between incorporated cities and [unincorporated Maricopa County](https://www.maricopa.gov/1674/Residential-Construction), and some jurisdictions exempt like-for-like replacement entirely. A roofer who cannot tell you which rules apply to your address has not done this in your city.

## What are the Arizona-specific warning signs?

Four, and they cluster after storms.

**Door knocking after a monsoon event.** Legitimate Arizona roofers are booked solid after a major storm. They are not driving neighborhoods. After the [August 9, 2026 wind event that tore roof sections off homes across the West Valley](https://www.weather.gov/psr/August92026), the crews knocking doors in Avondale and Buckeye were overwhelmingly out-of-state. See [storm chaser roofers in Arizona](/blog/storm-chaser-roofer-arizona/).

**Insurance benefit assignment at the door.** Signing an AOB hands your claim to a contractor. Never sign one on a first visit.

**Large deposits.** Material deposits are normal. Half the contract price before anyone shows up is not.

**Pressure tied to your deductible.** An offer to "waive" or absorb your deductible is asking you to participate in insurance fraud, and it is a reliable indicator of everything else about the operation. Our guide to [Arizona roofing scams](/blog/roofing-scams-arizona/) covers the full pattern.

## What if the work goes wrong anyway?

Document immediately and move fast, because your clock started the day the work was performed.

Photograph the condition, keep every text and email, and put your complaint to the contractor in writing so there is a record of the attempt. If that fails, file with the ROC inside the two year window. If property beyond the roof was damaged, our guide to [what to do when a roofer damages your property](/blog/roofer-damaged-my-property/) covers the separate liability path.

The technical standard matters in a dispute. The [NRCA's technical resources](https://www.nrca.net/roofing-guidelines/resources) define what competent installation looks like, and an independent inspection referencing those standards carries more weight than your description of the problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I check if an Arizona roofer is licensed?**
Search the Registrar of Contractors license database by business name or license number. Confirm the status is active, the classification covers roofing, and the licensed name matches the name on your contract exactly.

**Is a BBB rating enough to trust a roofer?**
No. BBB accreditation is a paid membership and reflects complaint handling, not legal qualification. Use it after the ROC check, and read the negative reviews rather than the star average.

**How many bids should I get for a roof in Arizona?**
Three, on identical scope. Different scopes produce prices that cannot be compared, which is exactly how a low bid wins work it should not.

**Should I hire the roofer my insurance company recommends?**
You are not required to. Preferred vendors are convenient and often fine, but they have an ongoing relationship with the insurer, not with you. Verify them through the ROC like anyone else.

**How long do I have to complain about roofing work in Arizona?**
Two years, per the Registrar of Contractors, measured from close of escrow or occupancy on new construction, or from the date of the work on repairs and remodels. Do not wait for a contractor to stop returning calls before filing.
