# Can You Put New Shingles Over Old Shingles?

**Yes, in most Arizona jurisdictions you can legally install one new layer of shingles over one existing layer, and a layover typically saves 15 to 25 percent versus a full tear-off. In Arizona you usually should not. The component that actually fails on desert roofs is the underlayment beneath the shingles, and a layover covers it without touching it. You end up paying for a new roof surface while leaving the failing part of the system in place.**

This is one of the few roofing questions where the national answer and the Arizona answer differ on reasoning rather than on rules. The codes are similar. The climate is not.

## Can you legally put new shingles over old shingles?

Generally yes, up to two total layers. That two-layer ceiling comes from the International Residential Code, which Arizona jurisdictions adopt with local amendments.

Chandler is a useful example because the city publishes its rule in plain language. Its [Homeowner Building Permit Manual](https://www.chandleraz.gov/sites/default/files/departments/development-services/City-of-Chandler-Home-Owners-Building-Permit-Manual.pdf) states you need a permit to re-roof when "replacing the old roofing with like material that already exceeds two layers." Chandler builds on the 2024 International Codes with published [local amendments](https://www.chandleraz.gov/sites/default/files/departments/development-services/City-of-Chandler-Amendments-to-the-2024-International-Codes.pdf).

Rules vary by jurisdiction. Unincorporated areas follow [Maricopa County residential construction](https://www.maricopa.gov/1674/Residential-Construction) requirements, while incorporated cities set their own. Confirm locally before assuming, and see our guide to [permits required for roof replacement in Arizona](/blog/permits-required-roof-replacement-arizona/).

Legal and advisable are different questions, which is the rest of this page.

## Why is a layover a worse idea in Arizona than in most states?

Because of what fails first here.

In wet, freeze-thaw climates, shingles usually fail from the top down: granule loss, cracking, wind damage. Covering a tired surface with a fresh one at least addresses the thing that was failing.

In Arizona the failure runs the other direction. The [NWS Phoenix office documents the sustained extreme heat](https://www.weather.gov/psr/heat) the low desert absorbs each summer, and attic and deck temperatures run far above ambient. That heat degrades the underlayment, the waterproof membrane between the shingles and the deck. Underlayment is what actually keeps water out. Shingles are the wear surface protecting it.

A layover does not replace underlayment. It cannot, because the old shingles stay in place on top of it. So the layer doing the waterproofing keeps aging while you pay for new shingles above it. Our piece on [why underlayment matters](/blog/roofing-underlayment-importance/) covers the failure mode in detail.

Adding a second shingle layer also traps heat between the layers, which does the new shingles no favors in a climate where thermal load is already the primary aging mechanism. The [EPA's cool roof guidance](https://www.epa.gov/heatislands/using-cool-roofs-reduce-heat-islands) explains how much of a roof's performance in hot climates comes down to managing heat rather than water.

## What does a layover actually save?

Real money, just less than people expect.

Tear-off is labor plus disposal. Skipping it typically saves 15 to 25 percent of project cost on a shingle roof. On a $14,000 replacement that is roughly $2,000 to $3,500. It also shortens the job by a day or two and eliminates dumpster logistics.

That is the honest case for a layover, and on a rental you plan to sell within a few years it can pencil out. What it does not do is buy you a full-life roof.

## What are you giving up?

Five things, and the first is the one that costs the most later.

- **The deck inspection.** Tear-off is the only time anyone sees your decking. Rot, delamination, and prior water damage are found and fixed then. A layover skips that entirely, so a soft deck stays soft under two layers of shingles.
- **Underlayment replacement.** Covered above. This is the big one in Arizona.
- **Warranty scope.** Manufacturer coverage assumes installation over a proper deck, and [Owens Corning's Duration installation instructions](https://www.owenscorning.com/en-us/roofing/install-instructions/duration-series) specify fastening and surface conditions accordingly. Many contractor workmanship warranties exclude layovers outright. See [what voids a roof warranty](/blog/what-voids-a-roof-warranty/) for how the two warranties differ.
- **Service life.** A layover roof commonly reaches 70 to 80 percent of what the same shingles would deliver over a clean deck, because the uneven substrate stresses the new course and the trapped heat accelerates aging.
- **Weight.** A second layer adds meaningful dead load. Usually within tolerance on a framed shingle roof, but it matters if anyone later considers tile, which requires an engineering analysis in cities like Chandler.

The [NRCA's technical resources](https://www.nrca.net/roofing-guidelines/resources) treat the deck and underlayment as defined system components rather than optional extras, which is the framework worth applying to this decision.

## When does a layover actually make sense?

Narrow cases, and they are legitimate:

1. **One existing layer, in flat and sound condition, with documented recent underlayment.** Rare, but it happens on roofs under 12 years old.
2. **A property you are selling within two to three years**, where disclosed layer count is acceptable to buyers in your market.
3. **A small, simple, low-slope-free roof** where tear-off logistics dominate the cost.

If your roof already has two layers, the question is settled. A third is not permitted, and stripping to the deck is the only path. For what each Arizona city publishes on the limit, see [how many layers of shingles you can have on a roof](/blog/how-many-layers-of-shingles-can-you-have-on-a-roof/).

## How do you tell how many layers you already have?

Look at a roof edge, usually at the rake or eave where the shingle courses terminate. Two distinct bands of shingle stacked at the edge means two layers. A roofer will also lift a shingle at a vent or valley to count.

Permit history helps. If a prior re-roof was permitted, the jurisdiction has a record. Absence of a permit record is not proof of a single layer, only that nobody filed.

Before you commission either approach, confirm your contractor is licensed at the [Arizona Registrar of Contractors](https://roc.az.gov/before-hire). A contractor pushing a layover on a 20 year old Arizona roof without discussing underlayment is telling you something about their standards. If you are still deciding whether replacement is warranted at all, start with [roof repair versus replacement](/blog/roof-repair-vs-replacement/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does a layover save compared to a tear-off?**
Typically 15 to 25 percent of total project cost, since you are removing tear-off labor and disposal. On a mid-range Arizona shingle roof that is roughly $2,000 to $3,500. Expect to give back some of that in shortened roof life.

**Can you put shingles over a tile roof?**
No. Tile and shingle are different systems with different attachment and load profiles. Converting between them is a full tear-off, and going the other direction, from shingle to tile, triggers an engineering analysis in many Arizona cities because of the added weight. See [tile versus shingle roofing in Arizona](/blog/tile-vs-shingle-roof-arizona/).

**Will a layover pass inspection in Arizona?**
If it stays within the two-layer limit and your jurisdiction permits layovers, yes. Requirements differ between incorporated cities and unincorporated Maricopa County, so verify before scheduling rather than after.

**Does a second layer void my shingle warranty?**
It does not cancel the manufacturer's product warranty, which covers material defects. It can put you outside the installation conditions that coverage assumes, and many contractor workmanship warranties exclude layovers by name. Get the exclusion language in writing before work starts.

**Is a layover a red flag when buying a house in Arizona?**
It is worth pricing in. Two layers means the next roof is a full tear-off with disposal of both, and it means nobody has inspected that decking in years. Neither is disqualifying, but both belong in your negotiation.
